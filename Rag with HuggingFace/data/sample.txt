Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy. Inside the chloroplasts of a plant cell, the pigment chlorophyll absorbs sunlight, primarily in the blue and red wavelengths, while reflecting green light, which is why most plants appear green. The absorbed energy drives a series of reactions that split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a byproduct and generating energy carriers used to fix carbon dioxide into glucose. This glucose can then be used immediately for energy or stored as starch for later use. Photosynthesis is the foundation of nearly all food chains on Earth and is responsible for maintaining the oxygen content of the atmosphere.

The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water within the Earth and atmosphere. Solar energy heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers, causing it to evaporate into water vapor. This vapor rises and cools in the atmosphere, condensing around tiny particles to form clouds in a process called condensation. When droplets in clouds grow large enough, they fall back to the surface as precipitation, in the form of rain, snow, or hail. Some of this water flows across the land as runoff into rivers and oceans, some infiltrates the ground to become groundwater, and some is taken up by plants and later released back into the atmosphere through transpiration. The cycle has no true starting point and repeats indefinitely, redistributing fresh water across the planet.

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape once it crosses a boundary called the event horizon. Black holes form when massive stars collapse under their own gravity at the end of their life cycle, compressing an enormous amount of mass into an extremely small volume. The point of infinite density at the center of a black hole is called a singularity, where our current understanding of physics breaks down. Although black holes themselves emit no light, they can often be detected indirectly, by observing their gravitational effects on nearby stars or by the intense radiation emitted by superheated gas as it spirals into the black hole in a structure called an accretion disk.
